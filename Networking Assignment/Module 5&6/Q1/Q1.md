## **ARP Packets — Capture and Analysis**

### **What happens during an ARP exchange**

When a device wants to communicate with another device on the same network, it knows the destination IP but not the MAC address. Since frames at the data link layer need a MAC address to be delivered, the device sends out an ARP request to find it. The target device then responds with its MAC address, completing the mapping.

### **Role of sender IP and MAC in ARP**

The sender's IP and MAC together are the core of how ARP works. When PC-A sends a request, it includes its own IP and MAC in the sender fields — this allows PC-C to update its own ARP cache with PC-A's details without needing to send a separate request. So a single ARP exchange actually updates both devices' caches simultaneously. The sender MAC in the reply is the actual answer being sought — it is what gets stored in the requesting device's ARP table and used for all subsequent frame delivery to that IP.

### **Importance of ARP in packet forwarding**

ARP is the critical bridge between Layer 3 (IP) and Layer 2 (MAC) in a network. IP routing decides where a packet needs to go, but the actual delivery of that packet across a local network segment depends entirely on MAC addresses. Without ARP, a device would know the destination IP but have no way to construct the Ethernet frame needed to send it.

Every time a packet is forwarded — whether from a PC to its gateway, or from a router to the next hop — an ARP lookup happens first. If the MAC address is not already in the cache, an ARP request goes out before the packet can move. This makes ARP fundamental to the day-to-day operation of any Ethernet network. It is lightweight, automatic, and works entirely in the background, which is why most users never notice it — until it is exploited through spoofing.
